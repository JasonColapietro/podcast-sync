Local voices: why Palm Beach County businesses should publish audio with transcripts

By Jason Colapietro, Suede AI · West Palm Beach, Florida · 2026-11-14

A Jupiter marina owner explains on a local podcast exactly how to winterize an outboard. A Palm Beach Gardens accountant spends 40 minutes on a webinar answering hurricane-season insurance questions. Neither answer will ever show up in Google or ChatGPT, because nobody can search what was only said out loud.

That is the gap this note is about. Jason Colapietro and the Suede AI team build websites and search visibility programs from West Palm Beach, and the most underused asset we find in local businesses is recorded audio that never became text.

Why audio alone is invisible

Search engines and AI answer engines index words on pages. Some platforms auto-caption video, but those captions live on YouTube or Spotify, not on your domain, and they rarely get cited as a source. When someone in Tequesta asks an assistant "who repairs hurricane shutters near me", the answer comes from text that names the service, the place and the business. A transcript on your own site is that text, in your own words, tied to your own URL.

There is a second reason. Local expertise is exactly what generic content cannot copy. A transcript of a contractor explaining Palm Beach County permit timing contains details no national blog has. That is the kind of page answer engines prefer to quote.

What we learned from this podcast's own pages

This site is a working example, warts included. The AI Suede podcast carries 33 recordings, and each now has its own page built from the feed, as described in our note on the episode page pattern. The pages carry show notes, a summary, related episodes and, for two episodes, YouTube's chapter markers. What they do not yet carry is a full transcript, and you can see the cost: an episode can rank for what its show notes say, but not for the dozens of specific answers spoken inside it.

Jason Colapietro's rule for the Suede estate is to publish what is true now and say what is missing. So here it is plainly: transcripts are the next layer for this feed, and the advice below is what we follow for client work in Palm Beach County.

A transcript workflow that holds up

  • Generate, then correct. Automatic transcription is good and cheap, but it mangles local names. Juno Beach, Loxahatchee, Tequesta and street names come out wrong often enough that a human pass is not optional. Budget roughly the length of the recording again for a careful edit.
  • One recording, one page. Do not dump twelve transcripts on a "media" page. Each episode or webinar gets its own URL, title and description, so each can rank for its own subject.
  • Lead with a written summary. Put three or four sentences at the top that answer the main question directly. Answer engines often quote the first clear answer they find.
  • Add chapters with timestamps. Chapters give readers a way to jump and give search engines the structure of the conversation.
  • Name speakers and credit guests. If a guest from a Boca Raton firm joins you, say who they are and link to them. Accurate credit is good manners and good schema.
  • Mark it up. Use PodcastEpisode or VideoObject structured data, point the author at one consistent Person record, and list the page in your sitemap.

Mention places the way people talk, not the way spammers do

Local SEO tempts people to stuff "West Palm Beach" into every paragraph. Do not edit a transcript to do that. If you mentioned Clematis Street or the Jupiter Inlet in conversation, keep it, because it is a real local signal. If you did not, add place context in the summary and the page title where it is true, and leave the spoken words alone. Edited quotes that read like keyword lists make the whole page less trustworthy to readers and to the systems that rank it.

The honest tradeoffs

Jason Colapietro is blunt about the downsides. Transcripts are long, and long pages can feel unreadable on a phone. Use headings and a summary so people can skim. Some recordings should not be published word for word: an off-the-cuff remark about a client, a price you no longer offer, or a guest who did not agree to a transcript. Get consent from guests before publishing, and cut what you would not put in writing. Finally, a transcript does not replace a good service page. It supports one. Link from each episode page to the service it relates to, and back.
