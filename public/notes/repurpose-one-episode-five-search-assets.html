Repurposing one episode into five search-ready assets without duplicate content

By Jason Colapietro, Suede AI · West Palm Beach, Florida · 2026-11-15

One hour of conversation can feed a website for a month, or it can be pasted into five places and compete with itself in every one of them. The difference is not how many assets you make from an episode. It is whether each one does a job the others cannot.

This note is how Jason Colapietro and Suede AI repurpose a single recording into five search-ready assets without publishing the same text five times. It draws on how this podcast site is built, and it is written for creators and small businesses around Palm Beach County who record far more than they publish.

What duplicate content actually costs

Search engines rarely punish a site for repeating itself. What they do is pick one version of the repeated text to show and quietly set the others aside. If the version they pick is a social post or a hosting platform's page instead of your episode page, the links, clicks and citations go there. Answer engines behave much the same way: when five pages say the same thing, the one that gets quoted is the one that looks most like the original, and that is often not yours.

So the rule at Suede is short. Every repurposed asset gets a different job, different words, and a link back to one canonical home.

Asset one: the episode page, the canonical home

The episode page holds the complete record: the show notes as written, chapters, the full corrected transcript when there is one, guest credits and structured data. On this site every episode page is generated from the feed and carries its own canonical URL, and a test fails the build if two pages share a title, description or canonical. Everything else points here.

The feed repeats the show notes, and that is fine. Podcast apps read an RSS item; search engines rank web pages. The feed item and the episode page are one piece of content in two formats, not two pages competing.

Asset two: an article that answers one question in new words

Pick the single most useful answer in the episode and write it up as an article. Do not paste the transcript section. Rewrite it for a reader: the claim, the reasoning, a worked example and what to do next, with a link to the timestamp where it was said. When Jason Colapietro does this, a 40-minute conversation about royalty splits becomes one focused article on how a split sheet works. The article ranks for the question; the episode page ranks for the conversation.

Suede AI's docs site applies the same thinking to reference pages in its note on building docs that AI assistants quote correctly: one source of truth, written so a quoted passage stands on its own.

Asset three: the video description and its chapters

The YouTube description is not a copy of the show notes. It is shorter, it leads with what the viewer will see, it carries the chapter timestamps, and it links to the episode page for the transcript. Two uploads on this channel had no chapters in their descriptions, so their episode pages show YouTube's automatic markers and say exactly where they came from. That label keeps the page honest about who wrote what.

Asset four: short clips with their own captions

A 45-second clip should answer one narrow question, with a caption written for the platform it lives on. Write one line that says what the clip answers and one that points to the episode page, rather than pasting the article's opening into every caption. Clips earn attention, not rankings. Their job is to send people to the canonical home, so their text can stay short.

Asset five: a hub entry that adds context

Topic hubs collect episodes on one subject. This site has five, including music royalties and AI and the music industry. A hub entry carries a short summary taken from the episode's own notes and a link, never the notes in full, and the hub adds what no single episode can: the whole list on one subject in one place. An episode joins a hub only when its own notes cover the subject, and a hub with fewer than four episodes fails the build rather than shipping as a thin page.

The one deliberate exception

One file on this site repeats nearly everything on purpose: llms-full.txt, a single Markdown file with every episode's show notes, the hubs and these notes, for language models that would rather read one file than thirty pages. Its sections name the URL they came from, so the copy points back to the canonical page instead of competing with it. It is a text file, not an HTML page, and the build Jason Colapietro maintains writes it, so it cannot drift from the pages it summarises.

A quick test before you publish

  • Could a reader tell from the first two sentences why this asset exists separately?
  • Does it link to the canonical episode page?
  • Is more than a paragraph identical to text on another URL you control? If so, cut it or rewrite it.
  • Does each web page's canonical tag point at itself, and does only the episode page carry the full transcript?
