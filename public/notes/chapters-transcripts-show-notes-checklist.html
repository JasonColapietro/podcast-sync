Chapters, transcripts and show notes: a production checklist for Delray Beach and Boynton Beach podcasters

By Jason Colapietro, Suede · West Palm Beach, Florida · 2026-11-15

Picture a Delray Beach podcaster who records a strong hour on how restaurants along Atlantic Avenue survive the summer slowdown, uploads it, pastes two lines into the description box and moves on. Months later the episode still has no search visibility at all, because the only text on the web about it is those two lines.

This checklist is the production pass Jason Colapietro and Suede run before an episode goes live. It is written for the independent shows in Delray Beach and Boynton Beach, from chamber interviews to fishing reports out of the Boynton Inlet, but it works for any show that wants its recordings found and quoted.

Why show notes, chapters and transcripts are three different jobs

The three layers are often treated as one chore. They have three different readers. Show notes serve the person deciding whether to press play and the podcast app that displays them. Chapters serve the listener who wants minute 23, and the search engine that wants the shape of the conversation. The transcript serves everyone who will never press play: the skimmer, the reader who relies on text, and the answer engine looking for a sentence to quote.

When one layer is missing, the others cannot cover for it. This site shows the gap plainly. Of the 33 recordings in the AI Suede feed, one carries chapter markers in its own show notes and two carry markers YouTube generated automatically, labelled as such on the page. None has a full transcript yet, so every episode page can rank for what its notes say and nothing more.

Before you record

  • Write the one-sentence answer first. Decide which question the episode answers and write the answer in one sentence. It becomes the first line of the show notes and the top of the transcript page.
  • Collect spellings. Guest names, business names and places. Automatic transcription is weak on local names: Boynton can come back as "Boyington", Lake Worth Beach loses a word, and a guest's surname turns into a common noun. A one-page spelling sheet saves an hour of editing.
  • Get consent in writing. Ask every guest whether a full transcript can be published. Asking before the recording is a far easier conversation than taking a page down afterwards.

Show notes checklist

  • Open with the answer sentence, not "In this episode we talk about".
  • Name the guest, their role and their business, and link to them once.
  • Keep the summary that doubles as a meta description between 120 and 160 characters, cut on a word boundary. The generator Jason Colapietro wrote for this site cuts descriptions into that range, and a test fails if any page drifts outside it.
  • Strip markdown asterisks and emoji bullets from anything a search engine will display. The first version of these episode pages shipped raw asterisks into descriptions; the fix was a cleaning step, not a rewrite.
  • Mention places only where the conversation was about them. An episode on events at Old School Square should say Delray Beach. A general marketing episode should not pretend to be local.

Chapters checklist

  • Put timestamps in the description in one consistent format, starting at 00:00, so both YouTube and podcast apps can read them.
  • Title each chapter as the question it answers, so the list reads like a table of contents someone could search.
  • Aim for one chapter every five to ten minutes. Thirty chapters in an hour is noise.
  • If you rely on automatic chapters, say so. The pages here read "YouTube generated these chapter markers", because a reader deserves to know nobody on the show chose those titles.
  • Link each timestamp to that moment in the recording. Every chapter on this site opens the YouTube upload at that point.

Transcript checklist

  • Generate with a speech-to-text tool, then edit with the spelling sheet open. The editing, not the generation, is where the time goes.
  • Label speakers by name, never "Speaker 1".
  • Break the text under the chapter titles as headings, so the transcript and the chapters share one outline.
  • Put a three-sentence written summary above it. Suede Signal's guide to writing passages AI answer engines can quote explains why the first clear answer on a page carries so much weight.
  • Remove what you would not put in writing: an old price, an aside about a client, a guest's unguarded moment.

Publishing checklist

  • One episode, one URL, on your own domain. A transcript that lives only on a hosting platform builds that platform's authority, not yours.
  • Add PodcastEpisode structured data, with the author pointing at a single Person record by @id. Suede Signal's piece on how JSON-LD helps AI engines understand your brand covers the identity side in more depth.
  • Credit guests accurately. On this site, recordings made on other people's shows credit their host as producer and Jason Colapietro as a participant, never as the author.
  • Add the page to your sitemap and, if you publish one, to llms.txt.
  • Link the episode to the service or topic page it supports, and link back.

Where a small show should spend its time

The transcript edit is the expensive step, and the first few passes are the slowest, before the spelling sheet and templates exist. A weekly show does not have to do all of it every week. Notes and chapters are cheap enough for every episode; full transcripts can be kept for the episodes that answer questions people actually search. That is a fair tradeoff. A transcript of a rambling catch-up episode helps nobody, and at Suede we would rather see ten corrected transcripts than fifty raw ones.
